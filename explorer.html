A. Hypothesis direction and the relationship among p-values

The alternative hypothesis determines which tail area is used for the p-value. In a real study, specify that alternative before examining the data. Here, use one fixed sample to understand the mathematical relationship among left-tailed, right-tailed, and two-sided p-values.

1. How are the three p-values related for one sample?

Keep one sample fixed: 30% of 200 measurements are clear, and the null proportion is 37%. This gives one fixed Z-score. We will examine the areas to its left and right, then connect them to the two-sided p-value. Switching the displayed alternative is a way to explore these areas, not a way to choose a test after seeing which p-value is smallest.

Keep fixed: 200 measurements; observed proportion 30%; null proportion 37%; α = 0.05.
Change: Display “less than,” “greater than,” and “differs from 0.37” in turn. Focus on the shaded areas and p-values, rather than the reject/fail-to-reject message.

1. Predict — before moving. The observed proportion is below the null proportion, so z is negative. Which area should be smaller: the area to the left of z or the area to its right? What should the two areas add up to?

2. Move and observe. Record the left-tailed p-value, the right-tailed p-value, and the two-sided p-value for this same sample. Check that z stays unchanged. Add the two one-sided p-values, then divide the two-sided p-value by the smaller one-sided p-value. What numbers do you get? Small discrepancies can come from rounding.

3. Explain — in your own words. Why do the two one-sided p-values add to 1? Why is the two-sided p-value twice the smaller one-sided p-value for this normal Z-test? Is it always twice the right-tailed p-value?

One sample, one Z-score: z ≈ −2.0504. The data and standard error stay fixed; only the area used for the p-value changes.

AlternativeNormal-curve areaP-value for this sample
p < 0.37 (left-tailed)PL = Φ(z)0.020162
p > 0.37 (right-tailed)PR = 1 − Φ(z)0.979838
p ≠ 0.37 (two-sided)P2 = 2Φ(−|z|)0.040324

Here Φ(z) means the area to the left of z under the standard normal curve. The symbols PL, PR, and P2 refer to p-values, not population proportions.

Relationship 1: PL + PR = 1
0.020162 + 0.979838 = 1.

Relationship 2: P2 = 2 × min(PL, PR)
0.040324 ≈ 2 × 0.020162.

The left and right areas cover the entire continuous normal curve, whose total area is 1. For the two-sided test, results at least as far from zero as the observed z count in both directions. Symmetry gives two equal tail areas, each equal to the smaller one-sided p-value.

Which one-sided value do we double? When z < 0, double the left-tailed p-value. When z > 0, double the right-tailed p-value. When z = 0, both one-sided values are 0.5 and the two-sided value is 1. It is therefore not always correct to double the right-tailed p-value—or any one-sided p-value without checking its direction.

These relationships apply to the continuous normal Z-test used here. An exact two-sided binomial p-value is not generally twice the smaller one-sided exact p-value. For a real analysis, report the p-value for the alternative specified in advance; do not choose the alternative with the smallest p-value.

B. Explore sample design → observed evidence → significance level

First explore how sample size affects the evidence for a fixed observed percentage (Activity 2). Next change the observed percentage while keeping sample size fixed (Activity 3). Finally compare how different significance levels turn the same evidence into a decision (Activity 4).

Learning order versus study planning: We explore significance levels last to separate evidence from the decision rule. In an actual study, choose the alternative hypothesis and significance level before examining the results. The sample-size activity uses hypothetical samples with the same observed proportion; planning a larger sample does not guarantee a particular observed result.

2. Does the same percentage mean more in a larger sample?

Connection: Start with sample design: how many independent measurements will we collect? To isolate sample size, compare hypothetical samples that all have the same observed percentage.

Compare hypothetical samples with 45% clear measurements each. Only the number of measurements changes. The difference from the 37% null claim stays at 8 percentage points.

Keep fixed: Two-sided test; observed proportion 45%; null proportion 37%; α = 0.05.
Change: Increase sample size n from 200 to 400, then 800. The observed proportion stays at 45%.

1. Predict — before moving. Would a result of 45% be more convincing evidence against 37% in a sample of 200 or 800 independent measurements? Give a reason before moving the slider.

2. Move and observe. Record the p-value at each sample size. Check the null standard error SE₀ (typical sample-to-sample variation under the null) and the distance of z from zero.

3. Explain — in your own words. Why can the p-value change even though 45% − 37% stays the same? Use the change in typical sample-to-sample variation to explain.

Larger independent samples have less sample-to-sample variation. The same 8-percentage-point gap is therefore more unusual under the null: SE₀ gets smaller, z gets farther from zero, and the p-value decreases. Increasing n from 200 to 800 halves SE₀ and doubles z. These are hypothetical samples with the same percentage; collecting more real data does not guarantee that the observed percentage will stay at 45%.

3. What if the sample result is closer to 37%?

Connection: Now keep the sample size fixed and examine how different observed results affect the p-value.

We are testing whether the population percentage of clear measurements differs from 37%. Compare two possible samples of the same size: one with 45% clear measurements and one with 40%.

Keep fixed: Two-sided test (a difference in either direction); 200 measurements; null proportion 37%; significance level α = 0.05.
Change: Move the observed proportion from 0.45 (45%) to 0.40 (40%).

1. Predict — before moving. Which sample result, 45% or 40%, would be more surprising if the population proportion really were 37%? Would you expect that result to have a smaller or larger p-value?

2. Move and observe. Record the p-value and decision at 45%, then at 40%. Also check whether the Z-score moves closer to zero or farther from zero.

3. Explain — in your own words. Which sample gives stronger evidence against the 37% claim? Use the two p-values to explain your answer.

The 40% sample is closer to the 37% null claim, so it is less surprising under that claim. The p-value increases from about 0.0191 to 0.3795, and the Z-score moves closer to zero. At α = 0.05, we reject the null for 45% but fail to reject it for 40%. Failing to reject does not prove that the population proportion is 37%.

4. Can the decision change when the data stay the same?

Connection: Finally, keep the sample and its p-value fixed. Compare the decisions made using different significance levels.

Keep the same sample: 45% of 200 measurements are clear. Change only the significance level α, the threshold used to decide whether a p-value is small enough to reject the null.

Keep fixed: Two-sided test; 200 measurements; observed proportion 45%; null proportion 37%.
Change: Click α = 0.10, then 0.05, then 0.01 (10%, 5%, then 1%).

1. Predict — before moving. Which threshold requires a smaller p-value before we reject: 0.10 or 0.01? Do you expect changing this rule to change the p-value calculated from the sample?

2. Move and observe. At each α, record the p-value and whether the test rejects or fails to reject. Compare the p-value with the selected α.

3. Explain — in your own words. Which decisions changed? Explain why changing the threshold can change the decision without changing the evidence from the sample.

The p-value stays about 0.0191 because the data, null claim, and alternative have not changed. It is below 0.10 and 0.05, so we reject at those levels. It is above 0.01, so we fail to reject at 1%. A smaller α requires stronger evidence to reject. In an actual study, choose α before examining the result.

C. Understand Type I and Type II errors

So far, we made a decision from one sample. Now imagine repeating the study with a new independent sample each time. Depending on what is actually true in the population, a decision can be correct or mistaken.

Type I error: rejecting a null claim that is actually true

Suppose the population really has 37% clear measurements. If our sample leads us to reject that claim, we have made a Type I error. We report evidence of a difference when there is no real population difference. This is sometimes called a false alarm. The red area in the top curve shows how often this happens under the null model.

Type II error: failing to detect a real population difference

Now suppose the population really has 47% clear measurements, so the 37% null claim is false. Some samples still do not give enough evidence to reject it. Failing to reject this false null claim is a Type II error. The real difference exists, but the test does not detect it. This is what “missing a difference” means; it does not mean observations are missing from the dataset.

Power is the chance of correctly rejecting the null in this specified 47% scenario. The orange area in the bottom curve is Type II error (β); the green area is power (1 − β). Together they account for all possible decisions under that scenario.

Decision from the sampleNull true: actual p = 37%Null false: for example, actual p = 47%
Reject the 37% null claimType I error (α)Correct detection (power)
Fail to reject the 37% null claimCorrect decision (1 − α)Type II error (β)

Failing to reject means “not enough evidence against the null”; it does not mean “the null has been proved true.” Type I and Type II error rates describe repeated studies under different assumed population truths. We usually do not know whether a particular decision is an error because we do not know the true population proportion.

Use Activities 5–7 in this order: explore why a smaller real population difference is harder to detect; see how a larger sample can help; then examine the trade-off created by a stricter significance level. All three activities use a two-sided test.

5. Is a smaller real difference harder to detect?

Connection: First establish what it means to detect a real difference: compare two possible population proportions while keeping sample size and the decision rule fixed.

Keep testing the 37% null claim. Compare two possible population truths: 47% clear and 40% clear. These are imagined population percentages, not the observed percentage in one sample.

Keep fixed: Two-sided test; 200 measurements per study; null proportion 37%; α = 0.05.
Change: Move “Suppose actual p is” from 0.47 (47%) to 0.40 (40%).

1. Predict — before moving. Which difference from 37% would be harder to detect: 47% or 40%? Would you expect more studies to fail to detect the smaller difference?

2. Move and observe. At both settings, record Type II error β and power. Watch the orange area in the bottom curve. Check that the top curve and dashed decision cutoffs stay in place.

3. Explain — in your own words. Which population scenario produces more failures to detect a real difference? Explain why the same decision rule can detect a large difference more often than a small one.

When the actual proportion changes from 47% to 40%, the bottom curve moves closer to the 37% null curve. More possible sample results fall between the decision cutoffs, where we fail to reject. Type II error therefore increases and power decreases. The top curve and its false-alarm rate stay the same because the null claim, sample size, and significance level have not changed.

6. Can a larger sample help us detect a real difference more often?

Connection: Now keep the real population difference fixed and ask whether collecting more observations helps us detect it.

Now keep the hypothetical actual population proportion at 47%. Compare repeated studies that collect 200, 400, or 800 independent measurements each. We want to detect the same real difference from the 37% null claim.

Keep fixed: Two-sided test; null proportion 37%; hypothetical actual proportion 47%; α = 0.05.
Change: Increase sample size n from 200 to 400, then 800.

1. Predict — before moving. Do you expect a larger sample to detect the real difference more often? Can this happen while the false-alarm rate remains at the chosen 5% level?

2. Move and observe. At each n, record Type I error, Type II error β, and power. Notice whether the curves become narrower. Focus on the error rates; the observed percentage from a single sample is not used here.

3. Explain — in your own words. As sample size increases, does the test detect the real difference more often? Explain what happens to Type II error and power while α stays at 0.05.

Larger independent samples vary less, so the two population scenarios become easier to distinguish. Type II error decreases and power increases, while the false-alarm rate stays at 5% in this normal-approximation model. In the next activity, compare this improvement with what happens when we lower α instead of increasing sample size. Activity 2 asks about evidence from one sample; this activity asks how often the test succeeds across repeated studies.

7. What do we gain and lose with a stricter rule?

Connection: Finally, keep the population difference and sample size fixed. Ask what we gain and lose by requiring stronger evidence before rejecting.

Imagine repeating the study many times. The top curve assumes the null is true: 37% are clear. The bottom curve assumes a real difference: 47% are clear. We apply the same decision rule in both situations.

Keep fixed: Two-sided test; 200 measurements per study; null proportion 37%; hypothetical actual proportion 47% for the bottom curve.
Change: Click α = 0.10, then 0.05, then 0.01. Optional: move the cutoff slider afterward to explore more thresholds.

1. Predict — before moving. If we require stronger evidence before rejecting, will false alarms become more or less common when the null is true? Might we also fail to detect a real difference more often when the population proportion is 47%?

2. Move and observe. For each α, record Type I error (false alarms), Type II error β (failing to reject a false null claim), and power (detected differences). Compare the red area in the top curve with the orange and green areas in the bottom curve.

3. Explain — in your own words. As α gets smaller, what happens to Type I and Type II errors? Compare this with increasing sample size in Activity 6. Which change helps detect a real difference more often while keeping α fixed?

A smaller α makes rejection harder: the dashed cutoffs move outward. Under the 37% null, fewer results lead to rejection, so false alarms decrease. Under the 47% scenario, more results fall between the cutoffs and fail to reject, so failures to detect a real difference increase and power decreases. This trade-off holds here because sample size and the hypothetical actual proportion stay fixed. Unlike lowering α, increasing sample size in Activity 6 reduced Type II error and increased power while keeping α at 0.05.
