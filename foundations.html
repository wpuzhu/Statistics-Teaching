Where the one-proportion Z-test comes from, and when to trust it

Purpose of this page: the probability foundations behind the z-test. Four views: the binomial model for counts (pre-work), the Central Limit Theorem for a sample mean (pre-work), the CLT applied to sample proportions, which are sample means of 0/1 data (Lecture 1), and the exact binomial test vs. the z-test (Lecture 1, an extension that is not tested). The z-test itself, the influence of each component, and Type I/II errors are explored in the main page, Hypothesis_Testing_Proportion.html.

Show explanation

For n = 2 the means are still right-skewed, because the population is. By n = 30 the histogram is close to the normal curve, and by n = 100 it is nearly indistinguishable from it: this is the Central Limit Theorem. The centre stays at μ = 10 (x̄ is unbiased), and the spread shrinks like σ/√n: about 7.1 at n = 2, 1.83 at n = 30 and 1.0 at n = 100. A flat population needs an even smaller n to look normal; the more skewed the population, the larger n must be.

With p = 0.05 and n = 200, np = 10, right at the rule-of-thumb boundary; a slight right skew remains. In every case the simulated mean is near p (unbiased) and the simulated SD near the formula.

Exact two-sided p-value: total probability of all counts no more likely than the observed one (the rule used by R’s binom.test and SciPy’s binomtest). The Z-test uses no continuity correction.

Investigate. (1) Campus at α = 0.01: do the methods agree? (2) Rare event: by what factor does the Z-test understate the p-value? (3) Mendel: both methods fail to reject. Does that prove the 3:1 ratio?
Show explanation

(1) No: Z gives 0.0062 (reject) and exact 0.0112 (fail to reject); α lies between them. (2) About 7 times (0.0065 vs 0.0457): np₀ = 0.8, so the normal curve misrepresents the skewed tail. (3) No. The data are consistent with 3:1, but other ratios close to 0.75 are consistent too; failing to reject is not proof.
